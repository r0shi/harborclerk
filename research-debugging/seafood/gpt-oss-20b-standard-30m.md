## 1.  Aquaculture’s Environmental Footprint  

| Finding | Source |
|---------|---------|
| **Disease spread** – Aquaculture facilities can introduce pathogens to surrounding wild fish populations. | [AoE‑85‑text, page ?] |
| **Antibiotic use** – Farmed fish are often fed pellets containing industrial chicken by‑products, which can release hormones and antibiotics into the water column. | [AoE‑85‑text, page ?] |
| **Escape and inter‑breeding** – Escaped domesticated fish may interbreed with local stocks, diluting the genetic base that has helped wild populations survive. | [AoE‑85‑text, page ?] |
| **High feed conversion** – It takes roughly five pounds of wild fish to produce one pound of farmed fish, indicating a high feed‑to‑product ratio. | [AoE‑85‑text, page ?] |
| **Market dominance of farmed shrimp** – Farmed shrimp flooded the U.S. market, capturing 90 % of the wild fisheries’ share within two years after the 2001 EU crackdown on chloramphenicol. | [Shrimp outline2, page ?] |
| **Price advantage** – Farmed shrimp are less than a third of the price of wild shrimp, encouraging over‑harvest of the latter. | [Shrimp outline2, page ?] |

> **Implication** – While aquaculture can relieve pressure on wild stocks, the environmental costs (disease, antibiotics, genetic dilution) are non‑trivial and must be mitigated through better management and certification.

## 5.  Comparative Assessment of Approaches  

| Approach | Strengths | Weaknesses | Representative Evidence |
|----------|-----------|------------|--------------------------|
| **Wild capture (shrimp)** | Higher sustainability rating; natural disease dynamics; less antibiotic use. | Market share limited; susceptible to over‑harvest. | [Shrimp outline2, page ?] |
| **Farmed shrimp** | Low price; high market share; economic viability. | Disease spread; antibiotic use; escape; genetic dilution. | [Shrimp outline2, page ?]; [AoE‑85‑text, page ?] |
| **Mussel aquaculture** | Low feed conversion; natural filtration; environmental monitoring. | Susceptible to runoff and bacterial spikes; requires careful closure management. | [mussels14 WY, page ?] |
| **Fish farm (Veta la Palma)** | Holistic, sustainable model; integrates wildlife sanctuary; economic success. | Limited scalability; requires specific environmental conditions. | [veta la palma 4clean, page ?]; [VetalaPalmaRevision2LAA, page ?] |

> **Overall** – Sustainable seafood is most achievable when aquaculture practices are coupled with rigorous environmental monitoring, certification, and policy support. Wild capture remains preferable for species where it can be managed responsibly, but aquaculture can play a key role if best‑practice models (e.g., mussels, Veta la Palma) are adopted more widely.

## 6.  Recommendations for Harbor Clerk  

1. **Promote certification** – Encourage seafood suppliers to obtain recognized sustainability certifications that enforce feed‑source transparency and antibiotic limits.  
2. **Support mussel and Veta‑style farms** – Highlight these models as best practices in marketing materials and procurement guidelines.  
3. **Monitor runoff** – Require regular water‑quality testing for mussel farms, especially during rainfall closures.  
4. **Advocate for policy** – Support regulations that limit antibiotic use in aquaculture and protect wild stocks from disease spill‑over.
