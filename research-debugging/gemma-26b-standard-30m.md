### **Europe**

#### **France**
The corpus details several distinct French regions, each with unique terroir and production styles:
* **Bordeaux (Médoc):** The Médoc is often characterized by large-scale production of oaky wines designed to appeal to critics and foreign investors [jaugaret3WY, page ?]. However, specific producers like Jaugaret offer a counter-tradition, using very old vines (50-year-old Cabernet Sauvignon/Merlot and 100-year-old Malbec) to produce rustic, high-acidity wines that differ from the region's standardized, "heavily made-up" profiles [jaugaret1, page ?]. Within the Médoc, sub-profiles vary: Pauillac is richer, Margaux is delicate, St-Estèphe is rustic, and St-Julien features high-pitched notes [jaugaret3WY, page ?].
* **Burgundy:** Winemaking traditions are heavily influenced by vineyard classifications, where Grand Cru and Premier Cru designations result in different wine characters even from the same producer [terroir 8, page ?].
* **Loire Valley:** In this region, Vouvray wines are noted for being more concentrated and better suited for long-term aging than Montlouis wines [chidaine1, page ?]. Biodynamic traditions in Montlouis, such as those of Francois Chidaine, produce rich, exotic wines with notes of fig, melon, and dried honey [chidaine1, page ?].
* **Muscadet (Sèvre et Maine):** This region produces complex dry wines using the Melon de Bourgogne grape, which is named for its slight musky aroma [muscadet-2WY-1-1, page ?]. The terroir is defined by granite, schist, gneiss, and gabbro [muscadet 4WY, page ?]. Mature Muscadets (20–30 years old) are characterized by high acidity and mineral character, comparable to fine Alsatian Rieslings [muscadet 4WY, page ?].

#### **Germany**
* **Mosel Valley:** This region maintains a 2,000-year-old tradition of steep slate viticulture, primarily focused on Riesling [AOE_Mosel, page ?]. However, the tradition faces modern threats, including an aging winemaker population unable to manage steep sites and a market shift toward quick-ripening varietals and lower prices [mosel 3, page ?].

#### **Italy and Slovenia**
* **Etna (Sicily):** Etna features a temperate climate due to its high elevation, contrasting with the heat of sea-level Sicily [outis 4, page ?]. The region lacks a continuous style but produces wines with significant mineral depth due to highly ferrous, volcanic soils [outis 4, page ?; outis 4WY, page ?].
* **Brda/Collio:** The Goriska Brda (Slovenia) and Collio (Italy) regions share vineyards across the border [rebula-raw-EB, page ?]. Traditions vary by producer; for example, Kabaj wines in Brda are influenced by traditional Georgian amphora winemaking [rebula-raw-EB, page ?].

#### **Spain and Portugal**
* **Rioja (Spain):** In the Rioja Alta subzone, higher elevations and cooler nights result in lighter-bodied wines [senorio 2, page ?]. Traditional farming in this area may involve minimal intervention, such as using only Bordeaux mixture [senorio 2, page ?].
* **Madeira (Portugal):** Production is defined by a unique heating process to create molasses and caramel aromas [madeira 5DS, page ?]. High-quality Madeiras are heated slowly in neutral oak casks for years, whereas lower-quality versions are heated in temperature-controlled steel vats for 90 days [madeira 5DS, page ?].

### **Australia**
