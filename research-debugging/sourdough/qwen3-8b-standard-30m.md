## Origins and Historical Context

The origins of sourdough bread are rooted in the natural fermentation process, which relies on wild yeasts and bacteria present in the environment and flour. This spontaneous fermentation is distinct from the use of commercial yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), and it is responsible for the unique characteristics of sourdough, including its tangy flavor and chewy texture [painaulevain5, page ?].

The historical development of sourdough is closely tied to the geographical and cultural contexts in which it was practiced. For example, the tradition of pain au levain is highlighted through personal experiences, such as James MacGuire's discovery of the Poilâne family’s bread in Paris, which was dense, chewy, and had complex fermentation flavors [painaulevain10, page ?]. This reflects the deep-rooted connection between sourdough and artisanal baking traditions in France.

## Regional and Cultural Influences

The evolution of sourdough traditions has been influenced by various regions and cultures. This suggests that sourdough has not only been a staple in European bread-making but has also been shaped by cross-cultural exchanges.

This highlights how migration and settlement have played a role in the spread and adaptation of sourdough traditions.

## Maintenance and Fermentation Practices

The maintenance of sourdough cultures is a critical aspect of the tradition. The corpus emphasizes the importance of regular feeding, with recommendations for feeding every 24 hours, with 48 hours being acceptable and 72 hours pushing the limits. Overly acetic or off-flavors indicate problems, and the culture should be fed using a white flour build recipe [painaulevain4, page ?].

For longer storage, a vigorous sourdough culture can be refrigerated, provided it is properly prepared. A portion of the culture, either from a dough made an hour before or a maintenance build, should be used. The culture can remain active for over two months when refrigerated [painaulevain5WY, page ?]. It is also recommended to remove the culture from the refrigerator in advance to allow it to reach room temperature, followed by a build using the build recipe, before using it as the "chef" for baking [painaulevain5, page ?].

The hydration level of the flour also plays a role in the fermentation process. Using a lower hydration flour leads to milder acidity and allows for less frequent builds, slowing the fermentation process [painaulevain10, page ?].

## Versatility and Culinary Uses

Beyond bread, sourdough cultures can be used for various recipes, such as fougasse, indicating its versatility in culinary applications [painaulevain4, page ?]. This reflects the broader role of sourdough in traditional and modern cooking.
