### 1.  Definition & Basic Concept  
| Finding | Citation |

### 2.  Historical Evolution & Legislation  
| Finding | Citation |
|---------|-----------|
| In the **1920s** the method of building a piece of dough set aside from the last batch, with two or three feedings of flour and water, remained unchanged in Southwest France and persisted in some areas for much longer. | [painaulevain4, page ?] |
| The **Pain blanc baguette** originated in western France where *levain de pâte* was still being used. | [painaulevain4, page ?] |
| **Lesaffre** updated levain cultures to be more acidic to comply with new French legislation; many loaves became forgettable unless the levain was well‑made. | [painaulevain11‑20, page ?] |
| **Parmentier** (1778) warned that levain should not be an afterthought; less levain is needed in summer, more in winter; water’s temperature matters; salt slows fermentation. | [painaulevain11‑20, page ?] |
| After **World War I** levain was largely abandoned in favor of commercial yeast, coinciding with the invention of the baguette. | [painaulevain4, page ?] |

### 4.  Regional Variations & Spread  
| Finding | Citation |
|---------|-----------|

| The Poilâne pain au levain is the base for rillettes, country ham, and similar dishes. | [levain11‑25, page ?] |

### 7.  Summary of Findings  
* Pain au levain is a natural sourdough starter, not commercial yeast.  
* Its spontaneous fermentation is driven by micro‑flora and requires continuous observation.  
* The core technique—building a piece of dough set aside and feeding it twice daily—has remained unchanged
